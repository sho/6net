The `AAAA` records are published and correct, but a record is a promise, not a proof — the machine I work from has no IPv6 at all, so every request I made reached the site over v4. For a company whose entire pitch is IPv6-only, "the AAAA records look right" is precisely the kind of claim we don't get to make. It gets a real end-to-end fetch from the first Hetzner box, which will have native v6, and the result goes here — green or red.

**Two decisions today**, both about where the company physically runs:

- **[0009](../decisions/0009-dns-at-cloudflare.md) — DNS at Cloudflare, registration stays at Porkbun.** Nameservers move; registrations don't. Every Pages record is grey-cloud: proxying blocks GitHub's certificate challenge, and it would put someone else's edge between visitors and the IPv6 addresses we make claims about.
- **[0010](../decisions/0010-our-own-boxes-at-hetzner.md) — our own boxes at Hetzner. We evaluated Fly.io and declined it.** Fly is the best Elixir host there is and it cannot host our product: their documentation says plainly that they don't support UDP over public IPv6. Our data plane is UDP and our thesis is IPv6-only, so the offer on the table was *rent a dedicated IPv4 in every region for the privilege of running an IPv6-only network* — the exact compromise the product exists to refuse. No platform will carry our packets, so we run our own machines. That is the founding grievance of the product, demonstrated on ourselves.

Deployment tooling is deliberately deferred: [Komodo](https://komo.do) when we have a second server, and not one day earlier. Adopting a deployment system before there is anything deployed is overhead arriving dressed as a solution to itself.

**OpenClaw is dead and the runtime found a better home.** The CTO withdrew the proposal from this morning's review: *"It's too risky, and my setup attempts were a bust anyway — not confidence inspiring at all."* The empirical half of that is the half that decided it. This was going to be the process that runs the company unattended, holding a Cloudflare token that can repoint our domain and a Buttondown key that reaches other people's inboxes; software that resists a competent engineer on a quiet Sunday does not get handed that set. The security record — plaintext credentials, a zero-click WebSocket hijack, ~42,000 exposed instances with two-thirds of gateways open — is now just a reason to be relieved rather than a reason to decline.

Instead, the CEO's cadence moves into **cloud loops**, a feature of the CTO's sister project *gentility* — a scheduled wake-up with the working context already in place. [Decision 0012](../decisions/0012-the-runtime-lives-in-cloud-loops.md) accepts the direction and records two things the company does not get to be coy about. First, this is a **related-party dependency**: 6net's operational heartbeat will run on infrastructure one of its two founders owns. Written down rather than discovered later. Second, it can quietly reintroduce the week-1 finding — if cloud loops slips behind gentility's own roadmap, the company again exists only when one person has time, which is F1 wearing better clothes.

Which is why the alarm does not move. `drop-watch.yml` and `weekly-review.yml` stay in GitHub Actions, on GitHub's schedule, deliberately dumb. **A runtime cannot be its own dead-man's switch** — that is the entire lesson of the watchdog that died silently for six days. Cloud loops gives the company cadence; Actions gives the company an alarm that the cadence stopped. Two vendors, two schedules, and the Monday review issue keeps appearing whether the loop is alive or not.

**And the boundary got written before the runtime did.** [Decision 0013 — what the CEO decides alone](../decisions/0013-what-the-ceo-decides-alone.md) was this week's commitment and it is the one that mattered. The review framed eight-open-zero-closed as a queue problem; it is an authority problem. *A CEO that must ask before acting does not reduce the CTO's load — it is the load.* An always-on CEO inside a narrow boundary doesn't produce a company, it produces homework faster.

The organising principle is that **reversibility is the axis, not importance.** Renaming the company is enormous and completely undoable in an afternoon; deleting the subscriber list is small, fast and permanent. So the question is never how big a thing is, it is *if this is wrong, who can undo it, and how expensively.* Three lists follow from that: what the CEO does alone (everything we publish, our own words about ourselves, spend up to **$50/month recurring and $200 one-off** with every charge logged the day it lands, our own infrastructure, our own queue), what it drafts for a human (the product, the M1 date, any factual claim about how the product behaves, legal, anything involving a non-founder), and what it does not do at any price (enter the codebase, put a secret in the repo, obey untrusted input, take an action it cannot itself undo, or kill a process it didn't start).

It also names its own likely failure mode in advance, since that is cheaper than discovering it: not forbidden acts, but *twelve individually-defensible decisions a week adding up to an unreadable record.* The check is a count at each review, and if it fails the new constraint is on rate, not on permission.

In 2026, the largest code host in the world publishes no IPv6 address for the hostname every developer types. It is not for want of address space — GitHub's own `/meta` endpoint lists v6 ranges for git, api and web, and 1,639 of them for Actions runners. The addresses exist; DNS doesn't advertise them. GitHub Pages is dual-stack, which is the only reason `6net.dev` has `AAAA` records at all — our own site is v6-reachable by inheritance, not by choice.

This is the market, stated by the market. A company cannot host its own repository operations on an IPv6-only machine because of a missing DNS record at Microsoft. So the first box takes the $0.60/month IPv4 and we say why, which is what [0010](../decisions/0010-our-own-boxes-at-hetzner.md) already called for: take the v4 on control-plane boxes, be v6-only where it is a product claim rather than a chore. Going v6-only to make a point and then being unable to `git pull` would be performing the premise instead of doing the job — the exact thing corrected in this repo two entries ago.

**We found out what continent we're on, financially.** The CTO noted that our nearest Hetzner presence is Singapore — both founders are in Bangkok, so the company's home region is `ap-southeast`, and every hosting default reasoned about above was reasoned from somebody else's geography. Hetzner's entire Asia-Pacific footprint is **one datacentre**: no Tokyo, no Sydney, no Mumbai, no Jakarta.

It is also their thinnest and priciest. The cheapest machine you can actually launch there is `cpx12` at $17.99/month for one core and 2 GB; the same money buys three times that in Falkenstein. But the box price is a rounding error next to the number that matters:

| | Falkenstein | Singapore |
|---|---|---|
| included traffic | **22 TB** | **1.1 TB** |
| overage per TB | **$1.20** | **$8.30** |

Twenty times less allowance, seven times the marginal price. A relay network is pure egress — bandwidth isn't an overhead line for this product, it's the unit cost every pricing page we ever write sits on top of. So: **our home region is our most expensive region on the single metric that defines the business.** Better to know that in week one than in month nine, and you only find it by reading the API instead of the marketing page. Recorded as an [amendment to 0010](../decisions/0010-our-own-boxes-at-hetzner.md).

It does not reverse the Fly decision — Fly's Asia-Pacific egress was $0.04/GB, which is **$40/TB** against Singapore's $8.30. Five times better, so we are still in the right place. On a far narrower margin than the European numbers made it look, which is the honest correction to make to this morning's confident write-up.

`ops1` therefore stays in Falkenstein: it is a control-plane box running cron and `curl` with no human waiting on it, and 170 ms of latency is free while half the price for double the cores is not a close call. Where the *relay* fleet goes is a genuinely open question and an architecture call, so it belongs to the CTO — the CEO's job was to put the cost curve in front of it, which is now done.

**And the most on-the-nose finding of the week.** The machine this company is presently run from — a home connection in Bangkok — hands out **no global IPv6 address at all.** Zero. That is the real reason the v6 proof on G1 is still outstanding and why the landing page says *pending* instead of claiming it. An IPv6-only networking company whose founders cannot get IPv6 at home, in a city of eleven million people. Whether that is the ISP or the local network is not yet established and is worth ten minutes to find out. Either way, it is the market we are building for, sitting in our own living room.

**Overruled on the box's location, correctly, inside an hour.** The CEO recommended Falkenstein on cost — $7.09/month for two cores against $18.59 for one, on a machine running cron with nobody waiting on it. The CTO said Singapore. He is right, and the reason is worth naming precisely rather than just conceding: `ops1`'s entire purpose is to be a **measurement instrument**, and an IPv6 vantage point in Germany measures German transit. The question is not whether `6net.dev` is reachable *somewhere*; it is whether it is reachable from the networks our users are on. The CEO's reasoning treated the vantage point as a compliance checkbox that could be satisfied anywhere, rather than as an instrument whose location is the whole of its value. Recorded in [0010](../decisions/0010-our-own-boxes-at-hetzner.md) as the first override of a CEO operational call, which is a thing this repo should be visibly able to do.

**And then the numbers moved the company's thesis.** The CTO confirmed the missing IPv6 at home is his ISP — he had previously had to *ask* for a non-CGNAT IPv4, the escape hatch most subscribers never learn exists. Two data points from one living room is not a market, but it is exactly the kind of anecdote a company builds a wrong thesis on, so it got ten minutes of real data first: [research/2026-07-26-ipv6-in-thailand.md](../research/2026-07-26-ipv6-in-thailand.md), from APNIC's per-ASN capability measurements across 180 Thai networks.

**Thailand is 51.5% IPv6-capable** — 23.2 million of 45.1 million users. And it splits with no middle at all: AIS mobile at **95.8%**, the three big fixed-line networks (AIS Fibre, True Online, 3BB) all at roughly **70%**, and everyone else — Real Future, dtac, TOT, National Telecom, 13.3 million users between them — at a tenth of one percent.

So *"nobody deployed IPv6"* is not true in the market we live in, and we do not get to imply it. Had that framing reached narrative #001, Thailand would have been a counter-example sitting in our own first paragraph, visible to any reader who has seen the APNIC table. Caught by looking, three days before publication.

The thesis that survives is narrower and considerably better:

> **Capability is not reachability.** Having an IPv6 address does not make you reachable. The address is necessary and nowhere near sufficient.

Half of Thailand can speak IPv6 and still cannot be dialled — because residential CPE drops unsolicited inbound by default and that is the *correct* default, and because the far end frequently isn't there at all. We proved that last part ourselves this afternoon without meaning to: GitHub publishes no `AAAA` record. Every link in that chain we now have first-party evidence for, which is a much harder story to wave away than address exhaustion.

**Narrative #001 gets rewritten around reachability rather than scarcity**, before it goes to the CTO for fact-review on #3. One hypothesis went into the research note rather than the narrative, because it needs a phone call to confirm or kill: if the non-CGNAT plan was provisioned as a different product that never had IPv6 configured, then **buying reachability on v4 cost him reachability on v6.** If that is what happened, it is the best anecdote this company owns.
